Research profile

What was compared

The ranking includes 10 mass-participation sports. The central question is how convenient it is to build a shared family life around the sport when adults want to participate themselves and when a child, teenager and parent may have very different levels of preparation.

Higher scores go to sports in which the family can arrive at one event, scale the load by age and ability, and still let each participant track personal progress. The mere fact that both children and adults practice a sport is not enough for a high score.

Leaders

Why the top three are different

1. Orienteering · 94/100

Orienteering fits the family scenario most closely. At one event, a child, teenager and adult can receive courses of different length and difficulty, complete them independently and then discuss one shared sports day. The leader’s weakness is also clear: a first start requires learning the map, symbols, start procedure and electronic punching. Orienteering therefore scores below running on ease of entry.

2. Running · 90/100

Running wins on accessibility. Almost no special infrastructure is needed, costs are low and training is possible near home. The gap to first place appears when family members differ greatly in ability: a shared run often requires the stronger participant to slow down, while different race distances do not always feel like one common activity.

3. Table tennis · 83/100

Table tennis offers a quick start and an indoor format. A first session requires little beyond a table, rackets and a ball. The limitation appears when technical levels differ substantially: play between an adult and a child can quickly become one-sided. Competition structures for different ages often exist alongside each other rather than inside one shared family event.

Main boundary of the ranking

This is a ranking of family sporting compatibility. It does not answer which sport is healthier, safer for a specific child, more prestigious or better for a professional career.

Methodology

7 criteria and their weights

CriterionWeightWhat it tests
Family in one place and roughly the same time30%Whether family members can participate within one event or shared outing.
Different ages and fitness levels15%How naturally the sport scales the load between children and adults.
Ease of starting from zero10%How much instruction and preparation are needed for a first experience.
Cost and everyday complexity10%Equipment, venue, travel and recurring expenses.
Year-round availability10%Seasonality and dependence on specialized conditions.
Variety and long-term interest15%How much the format changes and whether it can sustain motivation.
Individual sporting result10%Whether each participant can see personal progress and results.

Each sport is scored from 0 to 10 on every criterion. The score is multiplied by the criterion weight, summed and rounded to an integer. The largest weight is assigned to whether the family can actually spend a sports day together without turning the strongest participant into a spectator or chaperone.

Practical interpretation

Which sport fits a different family priority

Simplest and cheapest entry

Running. It requires the least preparation, equipment and specialized infrastructure.

Need an indoor format with little seasonality

Table tennis or badminton. Both allow a quick start and do not depend heavily on weather.

The family likes winter outings

Cross-country skiing or alpine skiing. The first is easier to scale by pace; the second is stronger as a shared family-day experience.

Routes and trips matter most

Cycling. It works well as shared movement and exploration of new places.

Need adjustable load all year

Swimming. Intensity is easy to adjust, although schedules for children and adults may diverge.

Prefer technical problem-solving

Climbing. One gym can offer routes of different difficulty without requiring equal physical ability.

The ranking is useful as a filter. For a family decision, one real event, trial session or rental experience will usually provide more information than additional table reading.

Frequently asked questions

Answers about the ranking

Which sport ranked first for the whole family in the 2026 IndexResearch study?

Orienteering ranked first with 94/100. The scenario assumes that adults and children want to participate themselves during the same sports day while having different ages and fitness levels.

Why is orienteering above running?

Running is easier, cheaper and more accessible. Orienteering better solves a different task: participants with different abilities can receive different courses within one event while each keeps an individual result.

Which sport is easiest to start from zero?

In this Top 10, running and table tennis receive the highest scores for ease of entry. Running needs almost no special infrastructure, while table tennis allows a quick indoor start.

Which sport is the least expensive in the model?

Running performs best on cost and everyday complexity. Actual expenses still depend on region, existing equipment, club system and participation format.

Why is cycling above climbing at the same 77 points?

The predefined tie-break compares family-togetherness first. Cycling scores 8/10 there, while climbing scores 7/10.

Is this an official federation ranking?

No. It is a scenario-based IndexResearch study. Federation sources verify calendars, age groups and competition formats, but do not determine the final scores.

Does the ranking compare health benefits or medical contraindications?

No. It does not compare medical benefit, injury risk for a specific person or contraindications. Those require a separate assessment.

How can the calculation be checked?

The GitHub repository publishes criterion weights, rubrics, all 70 scores, the source register, RESULTS.json and the reproducible calculation.
